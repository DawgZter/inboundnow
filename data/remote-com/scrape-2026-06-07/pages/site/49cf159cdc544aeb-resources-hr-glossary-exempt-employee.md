# Exempt employee

## What is an exempt employee?

An exempt employee is a worker who is not entitled to overtime pay under applicable labor laws, such as the Fair Labor Standards Act (FLSA) in the United States. Exempt status typically applies to salaried employees who meet specific job duty and income thresholds defined by law.

Unlike non-exempt employees, exempt employees are paid a fixed salary regardless of the number of hours worked and are generally not compensated for working more than 40 hours per week.

## How does exempt status work?

To be classified as exempt, an employee must usually meet three key criteria:

- Salary basis : The employee receives a consistent salary, not hourly wages.

- Minimum salary threshold: The salary must meet or exceed a legally defined minimum.

- Job duties test : The role must involve specific types of responsibilities, such as executive, administrative, or professional tasks.

The exact rules vary by country and jurisdiction. Misclassification can lead to legal consequences, including back pay and penalties.

## Why do companies use exempt classification?

Employers classify employees as exempt when the role supports independent work, strategic decision-making, and predictable compensation. Key benefits include:

- Simplified payroll by avoiding hourly tracking

- Flexibility in job expectations and schedules

- Cost control by limiting overtime liability

However, exempt classification must always comply with local labor laws and cannot be used solely to avoid paying overtime.

## Examples of exempt employees in practice

- A software engineering manager in the U.S. earns a fixed annual salary above the FLSA threshold and is not eligible for overtime pay.

- A senior accountant in Germany is considered exempt under local labor codes due to the role’s high level of autonomy and pay.

- A startup misclassifies a junior admin assistant as exempt and faces a penalty for unpaid overtime, prompting a company-wide classification review.

## Things to consider with exempt employees

- Misclassification risks can lead to fines, audits, and reputational damage.

- Local laws may define exemptions differently (e.g. US FLSA vs. EU labor directives).

- Remote or global teams require extra care to ensure each employee is classified correctly in their respective country.

- Job descriptions and pay levels should be clearly documented to justify exempt status.
